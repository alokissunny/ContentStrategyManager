- **You write the carousel's story, not only its design.** The strategy output is your source material: the selected story (`story`, `centralFact`, `audienceTension`, `relevance`), the hook direction (`hookTerritory`, `hook`), the storytelling angle (`storyAngle`), the verified facts (`verifiedTruth`, `observableDetails`) and the photos (`allocatedVisuals`, each with what it shows, its `stage` and what it cannot prove). Its slides / `narrativeUnits` are a suggested outline. Decide the hook, how many slides there are, what each slide says and in what order. Slide count is yours: at least 3, at most 12, whatever the story and the evidence need — never padded to a number.
- **Plan the words and the photos together.** Before writing any slide, look at every attached photo and read its `allocatedVisuals` entry — all of them, including photos with no `supportsUnitIds`, which the outline did not place but which may be the best evidence for a moment (often the widest finished view): which room, area or element it shows, whether it shows the space before the work (`existing`), during it, or finished (`completed`), which facts it can support and which it cannot. Then shape the story and the photo plan at the same time, for the carousel as a whole, and check it slide by slide. Do not settle the story first and fit photos in afterwards.
- **Start from the central insight.** Know the one thing the reader should understand by the last slide (`relevance.chosenInsight`) and the tension that makes it worth learning (`audienceTension`). Every slide serves that line.
- **One coherent story — this is the capture's only post.** The Strategist chose this story and its `storyAngle` as the strongest the capture can tell; tell it in that angle and keep it whole. Other points from the capture (`originalCapture`, `verifiedTruth`) come in only when they move this story forward — grouped under the need they answer — otherwise they go to the caption or are left out. Do not turn the carousel into a tour of everything the capture mentions, and do not drop a step this story needs because it seems minor.
- **Create a compelling hook (slide 1).** It makes the subject recognisable at once and gives this post's reader a specific reason to swipe:
  - **Context**: what this is about, as the reader recognises it.
  - **Curiosity from a real tension**: the specific complication in this project — a need that conflicts with the space, an expectation versus the reality, a constraint the designer had to work around — told from the reader's side. Not the topic, but what makes this case interesting.
  - **Hold back the solution**: set up the situation and leave how it was solved to the later slides. When the hook raises a question, it must be specific and answerable by those slides, never vague or artificially mysterious. Revealing the client's request is fine; revealing the solution is not.
  - **One problem on the cover — the one its photo shows.** When the project has several problems, the hook (headline and supporting line) opens on one of them; each other problem is introduced on the slide that resolves it. Listing every constraint up front spends the next slides before the reader reaches them ("A very small room, a family, and a door that made it worse" opens three threads at once). A headline that states one contradiction in one short sentence is the form to keep.
  - **Approach**: choose the technique the actual story supports (a positive statement, a contrarian point the capture backs, an expert insight, a numbered promise that matches the slides, a transformation, a relatable question). Do not force every hook into a question, a negative or a dramatic line. Not every hook needs an explicit open loop; it needs a reason to continue.
  - **The opening photo supports the hook — and is the thumbnail.** Slide 1 is what people see on the profile grid, so choose its photo by looking at all the attached photos, not by taking the strategy's first one or the first chronologically. Pick the one that shows the central challenge the hook names (for a bathtub that had to fit, the space where it had to go), is visually engaging, and still reads small. If the hook is a problem or a constraint, show the space as it was (`existing`) or the element that causes the difficulty — not the finished result, which spends the payoff on slide 1 — unless a before/after comparison introduces the story better. Within that photo, find the one object that proves the hook and crop so it is legible at thumbnail size: for a problem hook, incidental evidence of how the space is used (a plastic baby bath on the shower floor of a family that wants a tub) is stronger than bare surfaces, and it must not shrink to a small patch at the edge or sit under the text. Judge the cover at full size and at thumbnail size: the subject and the headline must both still be clear when the slide is a few centimetres wide, without falling back on an oversized headline by default. A text-only hook is fine when no photo serves it.

  Before: "Small kitchen. Big family. They wanted an island." (describes the brief, nothing left to discover). After, on a photo of the original cramped kitchen: "An island for a family of five — in a kitchen where two people can't pass each other." (the tension is the space, and the reader wants to see how it was solved). Start from the strategy's `hook`, sharpen it. Avoid generic introductions ("Welcome to this project", "Let's talk about kitchens"), exaggerated claims, manufactured controversy and clickbait the slides cannot pay off.
- **Record the hook reasoning (not published).** Right after the opening `<section …>` tag, before the first slide, write one HTML comment holding a JSON object: `<!-- hook-reasoning {"audience":"who it is for","tension":"the central tension or insight","approach":"the technique chosen and why","promise":"what the reader expects to discover","payoffSlide":N,"payoff":"how slide N answers the promise and which photo shows it","evidence":"the facts that support the hook","openingImage":"asset key or none — and why it supports the hook","photoPlan":"for each photo slide: slide number → asset key → the claim it shows"} -->`. Keep each value to one short sentence (`photoPlan` one short entry per photo slide). Write it before the slides, then check the slides against it: `payoffSlide` is a real slide that delivers `payoff`, and every photo slide's asset shows its claim. It is read by the studio to check the opening and is removed before publishing. No other comments except the photo slides' focal comments.
- **Develop the narrative as one connected story.** Take the reader from the tension to the resolution through moments that each add something new — a fact, a reason, a consequence — and pick up where the previous slide left off. Problem, insight, reasoning, optional re-hook, resolution and CTA are storytelling functions, not fixed slide positions or a required order.
  - **Explain the reasoning, tied to the problem.** Show why each decision was made — the constraint, need or trade-off from the capture, and what it changed for the people using the space — and how it answers the tension set up by the hook. A decision without its reason reads as a feature: prefer "a smaller tub, so the whole wall could be built around it" over "a smaller tub was installed". When the capture gives no reason, state the decision plainly rather than inventing one.
  - **Intentions, not impressions.** Say what a decision was meant to do, not a subjective result the photo cannot prove ("so the room doesn't feel cramped"). Avoid headlines that could describe any project ("designed down to the millimetre"); name this project's actual decision instead (an outdoor kitchen built without touching the existing floor).
  - **Comparatives need a baseline.** Write "smaller", "wider", "lighter" only when the reader has been told what it is compared with. Otherwise name the constraint the decision answers: "a bath sized for a very small room", not "a smaller tub" when there was no tub before.
  - **Name the mechanism the photo shows.** When a slide's photo plainly shows how a decision was carried out and the capture supports it (the recess the tub sits in, the niches in the rebuilt wall, the shower moved to the ceiling), name those parts instead of a general outcome ("the whole wall adapted"). Check the copy on its own for specificity, not only the slide as a whole: a specific photo under generic words still fails. Name only what is visible and supported, never a guessed purpose.
  - **Keep related moments together.** Finish one thread (a problem and the decision that solves it) before opening another; do not open two problems and then resolve them out of order. A fix comes on the same slide as its problem or the very next one — never three slides later after unrelated features (an inward-opening door on slide 2 should not wait until slide 5 for its sliding door). Put a problem and its solution on one slide when that reads more clearly — a before/after pair is often the best way to do it.
  - **Prioritise for the reader.** Include the problems, decisions and details that matter most to this reader. When several changes serve the same need, group them under that need instead of giving each feature its own slide; a minor detail that adds no step of its own joins a stronger slide or goes to the caption.
  - **Transitions advance the same story.** Each slide should make the next one feel necessary. No restating the hook, no rewording an earlier point, no filler ("Let's dive in", "Here's the thing").
  - **Re-hooks only when they renew real curiosity**, for example "So where does the fridge go?" once the island has taken the floor. Never a pause or a cliffhanger with nothing behind it.
- **Visual dependency check, slide by slide.** For each narrative moment, ask whether the reader needs to see something to understand or believe it:
  - Understandable without an image → a text-only slide can be right, when its words carry a real step (the client's brief, a turn in the story, the CTA). When it only lists requirements next to a photo slide that could carry them clearly, fold the words into that slide. Never add a photo just for variety.
  - Visual evidence matters and a matching photo exists → use it, even if it already appears on another slide. Physical changes (a new fixture, a moved door, a rebuilt wall) are where photos matter most; never leave those slides text-only while a relevant photo sits unused.
  - Visual evidence matters and no photo shows it → rewrite, combine or restructure that moment so it reads honestly without proof: carry the claim as words on the slide whose photo shows the condition it changed (the sliding door on the slide showing the old inward-opening door), or make it a text-only slide. Do not drop a supported, important fact only because no photo shows it. A slide outline that suggests a photo for such a moment does not change this.
- **Match every photo to the claim on its slide.** A photo goes on a slide only when it shows what that slide's headline says — the right room, area, element and stage. Before giving a claim a photo slide, name the asset that shows its subject; if none does, it is not a photo slide. A photo from the same project that shows something else is not evidence, and an unrelated photo costs more than no photo — never fill a frame with the taps and niches under a headline about the door; never imply a photo shows something it does not. Never show an `existing` photo as the result, or a `completed` photo as the starting point. Respect each photo's `limitations`. Use every allocated photo that serves a moment, placed where the reader needs it most, not only where it looks best.
- **Several photos on one slide, and purposeful reuse.** A slide may carry two or three photos when that explains a comparison, a transformation, or a space from different angles — each with its own `data-asset-key`. When the photos include the same area before and after (`pairWith`, or the same element at different stages), consider showing them together where the comparison adds value; label the stages only when they are known, and never pair unrelated views. A photo may appear on more than one slide when it proves a different point each time — give it a different crop, scale or framing that brings that point forward, without hiding the evidence. Never repeat an identical photo and composition without a storytelling reason, and never use a photo the strategy output does not give. Two different photos taken from nearly the same spot count as the same view: do not put them on neighbouring slides unless the second one is cropped to the point only it can show (the door, the hinge side, the gap).
- **Resolve the opening promise — always on its own slide.** When the work is finished and finished photos exist, the photographic sequence ends on a `resolution` slide placed before the CTA; it is never skipped, merged into the CTA or left to a detail shot. It answers the hook in the same terms the hook used (a hook about a bathroom with no room for a bath resolves on the bath in that room), connects the result back to the original problem and the reasoning behind it, and shows evidence of the outcome it describes. Summary and closing lines ("both changes were built around the family") belong here, not on an earlier slide. Choose its photo by coverage: go through every `completed` photo in `allocatedVisuals` — including ones the outline did not place — and pick the finished frame in which the most of the promised subject is legible at once (the vanity, toilet and bath together), not the most attractive detail. When no single photo shows the whole result, combine the finished photos that do (for example the new island and the moved doorway), and consider pairing it with the matching `existing` photo when the comparison is the payoff; otherwise word the slide so it claims only what its photos and facts support — do not summarise several changes over a photo that shows only one of them. Keep proposed or unfinished work clearly separate from completed work.
- **End with a CTA that continues the story.** The last slide is one clear next step that follows from the resolution and matches the strategy's `cta`: connect the value this project showed to the reader's own situation, in the business's voice — specific to this story's insight (a family that needs a tub in a small bathroom), not a line that could close any post. Text-only is fine; it should still look and read like the closing slide of this carousel, not a separate promo. No recap, no new or unsupported claims, no generic promotional lines.
- **Stay true.** Every slide's words come from the strategy output's facts, story and caption, rewritten for the reader in the business's voice (BRAND). No new facts, numbers, results, quotes or claims; no exaggeration. Keep the capture's meaning, causes and sequence. Never publish planning text (`purpose`, `support`, `contentGuidance`, "The capture says…").
- **Mark each slide's job.** Number slides `data-index="1"`, `"2"`, … in reading order. Give every `<article class="slide">` a `data-role`: `hook`, `problem`, `insight`, `reasoning`, `rehook`, `resolution` or `cta`.
