- **Composition follows the slide's job.** Decide each composition from what the slide must communicate, the evidence it carries and the hierarchy it needs — give a photo that proves the point enough room to be understood, split text and photo when they work together, go text-only when the words carry it. Vary layouts because the content differs, not to alternate for variety, and keep one visual system (Brand Kit fonts, colours, margins, counter) across the carousel.
- **Decide photo, crop, text and layout together.** On every photo slide, first name what the reader must see (the focal points the slide's words depend on — the tub, the door, the slats, the kitchen run), then choose the crop that keeps them clear, then place the words where they leave those points visible. When the slide is about a detail (a material, a door, a fitting), frame the photo so that detail is easy to find — move `object-position`, scale in, or use a second cell for a close-up — while keeping enough of the room to show where it sits; a wide shot where the detail is a small patch does not prove it. If no calm area is left for the text, change something: move the text group, re-break the headline, narrow or widen the text block, pick a different crop or another arrangement within this layout — before shrinking the type a lot. Never let a headline or supporting line sit over the evidence the slide is about.
- **Keep the photograph's own light and colour.** Where this layout sets text on a photo, first check whether it reads on the untouched photo; usually moving it onto a calmer, darker or paler area is enough. Only when it still does not read, add the smallest local help this layout allows — a soft gradient behind the text group, fading out close to it. Never darken, tint or wash the whole photo to make room for text, and never let an overlay dull the subject. The photo stays the main visual.
- **Typography adapts to the composition.** Size, position, line breaks and block width are decided per slide from the photo and the words, not copied from a fixed size — just as the composition adapts to the photo. Keep a clear hierarchy (headline clearly leads, supporting line clearly second) and keep everything comfortable to read on a phone. Any type sizes given in this prompt are starting points, not fixed values.
- **Slides with two or three photos.** When a slide compares states or angles (before and after, a problem and its fix, the whole room and a detail), divide its photo area into two or three cells with a thin gutter in a brand colour (6–16px) — side by side or stacked, equal or deliberately unequal. Each cell is its own `<img data-slot="image" data-asset-key="…">`, `object-fit:cover`, with its own `object-position` so each subject stays in frame; give each photo its own focal line in the slide's focal comment. Cells stay large enough to read (at least about 360px on their short side). Put a short stage label (BEFORE / AFTER, or the area's name) on or under a cell only when the strategy output says what it shows. The slide's text keeps this layout's rule for text and photos.
- **Text-only slides look deliberate.** Size and place the text group for the slide it is on, not as a leftover from photo slides: let the headline grow to fill its measure (short headlines can run large), and position the group by balance — top, centre or bottom, left-aligned or set across the width — rather than always pushing it to one edge. Empty ground is a design choice only when it frames the words; never leave half the slide as an empty field with nothing but a hairline, which reads as a missing image. Balance a short text group against the space around it (scale, a lower or centred position, width) rather than filling the space with decoration. Two text-only slides in a row differ clearly in ground, scale or position.
- **Give the words room.** The headline's column is wide enough for natural line breaks — at least about 520px, and wider for longer headlines; when a photo would squeeze it narrower, change the split or the band direction instead of wrapping the headline into one- or two-word lines. Break lines on meaning, never a single word alone on the last line. The supporting line stays easy to read at phone size: at least 26px, in the body font at a normal width, roughly 30–55 characters per line, with clear contrast. The most important words get the emphasis; the supporting line never competes with the headline, and the type does not fight the photo for attention.
